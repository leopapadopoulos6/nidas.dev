Server Room: three ways to light it.

One room, three directions. The layout and interactions are shared: racks are sections, patch-panel ports are links, the console on the crash cart dives into a 2D résumé, and the locked terminal guards /apps. What changes is the palette, type, light, materials, and motion. Every still below is a live render of the same throwaway Three.js prototype, built from code with no models or image textures.

Waiting for Leo's pick. The shell (Phase 3) starts only after approval.

The room (shared by all three)

A cut-away diorama seen from a fixed three-quarter angle. Nothing needs WASD or scrolling: every interactive object is visible from the home view and one click away.

  • 4 racks → sectionsAbout, Work, Projects, Skills. Hover lights the rack's LEDs and crown strip; click dollies the camera to it and slides in a detail panel.
  • Patch panel → linksGitHub, LinkedIn, mail, résumé PDF (and spares). Click a port: its cable flashes and a packet runs up into the tray. Ports are ~10 px targets from the home view, so in the shell the first click brings the panel close and the second picks a port (each with an HTML label).
  • Console CRT → about / résuméClick to dive: the camera pushes through the glass into a full-screen 2D interface (boot text, terminal, windows) where everything is easy to read.
  • Locked terminal → /appsPlaceholder for future private dashboards. Shows ACCESS DENIED, no auth yet.
  • Everything else is atmosphereCRAC unit, cable tray, an open floor tile, a clock, a fire extinguisher. They give scale and make the room feel used.

Motion notes

  • Camera. Fixed three-quarter rig with pointer parallax (±0.35 m) and a slow idle sway. Click a rack: a 1.2 s ease-in-out dolly to a front-on framing, then the panel slides in. Esc or Back reverses it. No scroll-jacking and no free orbit, so it's hard to get lost.
  • LEDs. About 600 LEDs in one instanced mesh, blinking from per-LED rate/phase/duty in the shader, so no per-frame JavaScript. Hovering a rack doubles its LEDs and lights its crown strip.
  • Cables. Every cable carries slow data pulses (a UV-offset band in the shader). Click a patch port: its LED strobes three times and its cable surges, so the link visibly "comes up."
  • Neon. The street sign flickers once or twice a minute; the ceiling tube stutters. Light shafts and the striped floor pool are cheap additive planes, not volumetrics.
  • The dive. 1.3 s push to the CRT, then 0.7 s through it: FOV narrows 34° → 20°, barrel distortion and chromatic aberration ramp up, scanlines rise, and the screen fills the frame before crossfading to the 2D UI, whose boot text types in. Exit plays it backwards.
  • Loader. BIOS-style POST lines driven by real asset progress. The 2D content is already in the HTML underneath.

Strengths

  • Matches the brief most directly
  • Richest mood; shows lighting, reflection and post skills
  • Cyan/magenta reads clearly on dark

Risks

  • Cyan + magenta is a familiar cyberpunk pairing; detail has to carry it
  • Floor reflection costs a second render (needs a quality toggle)
  • Dark: in-scene labels must live in HTML

Motion notes

  • Camera. Same rig as A, with springier easing (slight overshoot) on hover lifts and panel arrivals. Things bounce a little.
  • UV breathing. The blacklight tube hums at about 0.5 Hz; fluorescent surfaces (cables, tape, LEDs) breathe with it, and hovering an object pushes it to full fluorescence.
  • Wayfinding. Click a rack and the floor tape runs marching-ants from the cart to that rack before the camera moves. The room points the way.
  • Patch ports. The cable flashes through all four fluoro colours, then settles on its own.
  • The dive. The CRT glass bulges into a fisheye, then a chrome-barred window pops out of the screen with a scale overshoot. The 2D UI is a desktop of draggable, stackable windows.
  • Loader. A chrome progress dialog with a candy-striped bar.

Strengths

  • Most memorable and least expected for a network engineer
  • Pairs naturally with a Y2K windowed 2D UI
  • Fluorescent palette pops on phones

Risks

  • Can read as "nightclub" more than "NOC"
  • Transparent racks need careful sorting; heavier on overdraw
  • Pink/purple contrast needs checking for colour-blind users

Motion notes

  • Boot. The room draws itself: edges trace in, back to front, over about 1.2 s (dash-offset on the line segments), then the LEDs come up rack by rack.
  • Camera. Same rig as A. Hover puts targeting brackets around the object, HUD-style, instead of a glow.
  • Patch ports. A bright "packet" dot runs the length of the clicked cable into the tray, one per click.
  • The dive. No crossfade: the console's text is the same font and colour as the 2D UI, so the camera pushes in until the CRT text is page-sized and the DOM simply takes over. The seamless option.
  • Alarm. The only non-green colour is red: the door beacon, the denied screen, and any error state.
  • Perf. No lights, shadows or reflections. It's the cheapest to render and could run as full 3D even on mid-range phones.

Strengths

  • Most distinctive; reads "engineer" instantly
  • Seamless dive into the Matrix-style 2D UI
  • Fastest and lightest of the three

Risks

  • Monochrome is cold and can tire the eyes
  • Loses the "neon light spilling in" warmth from the brief
  • Wireframe can look unfinished if edge density isn't curated

Recommendation

Go with A, Night Shift, and borrow C's dive.

A is the brief: a server room at night with neon spilling in. It also shows the widest range of skills in a single frame (lighting, reflections, instanced LEDs, shader-driven cables, post-processing), which matters because the site itself has to prove the ability. Your two favourites, Bruno Simon and Jesse Zhou, both win on rich, lit, toy-like scenes rather than on effects, and A is the closest of the three to that.

  1. Keep A's room (palette, Tektur + JetBrains Mono, wet floor, window spill) as the 3D identity.
  2. Make the console the doorway to C. The CRT screen and the post-dive 2D UI use a phosphor sub-palette, so diving in feels like changing medium, not just zooming. The A/C seam happens inside the monitor.
  3. Ship a quality ladder from day one: no-reflection and no-bloom tiers for weaker GPUs, and the full 2D site for phones, reduced motion, no WebGL, and crawlers, as the brief requires.
  4. Avoid the generic-cyberpunk trap with specifics: real switch port LEDs, a patch panel numbered like a real one, believable cable runs, a CRAC unit. The network-engineer details are what make it yours.

If you'd rather be memorable than on-brief, pick B. If you want the fastest, most coherent build, pick C.
